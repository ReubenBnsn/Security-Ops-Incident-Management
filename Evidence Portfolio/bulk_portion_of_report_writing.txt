### Methodology

The investigation adopted a broad-to-specific, evidence-led approach, transitioning from signature-based detection to deep packet inspection (DPI) and finally to payload validation. This sequence was chosen to efficiently narrow the search space within the network traffic
before performing resource-intensive manual analysis.

**Initial Triage and Signature Analysis**
The process began with a review of Snort and Suricata alerts. These tools were selected for their ability to provide rapid, signature-based identification of known malicious patterns. By analysing these logs first, high-fidelity Indicators of Compromise (IoCs)—such as the
Pushdo C2 check-ins—were identified, providing immediate focal points (IP addresses and timestamps) for the subsequent PCAP analysis.

**Host Identification and Asset Mapping**
To ensure the incident was attributed to a specific physical asset rather than a transient IP address, Wireshark was used to perform DPI on the provided PCAP. The investigation prioritised DHCP traffic; by filtering for DHCP 'Inform' and 'Request' packets, the hostname
`FlashGordon-PC` was mapped to the internal IP `192.168.1.96`. This was further validated by examining the Ethernet II layer to extract the MAC address (`00:15:c5:de:c7:3b`), ensuring a definitive link between the network activity and the hardware.

**Traffic Analysis and Payload Extraction**
The investigation then shifted to HTTP traffic analysis to determine the infection vector. Traffic was filtered by the infected host's IP and protocol, with a specific focus on outbound GET requests for executable files (`.exe`). To distinguish between legitimate downloads
and malicious payloads, the hexadecimal stream was inspected for the `4D 5A` (MZ) signature—the mandatory header for Windows PE binaries. This technical marker provided objective proof that executable code was transferred over the network.

**Integrity Validation and Threat Intelligence**
To move beyond filename-based identification, which is easily spoofed by attackers, all extracted HTTP objects were moved to an isolated Linux virtual machine. The `file` command was used for initial classification, and SHA-256 hashes were generated using `shasum`. These

**Correlation and Synthesis**
The final stage involved correlating the timestamps of the initial JS delivery, the subsequent binary downloads, and the eventual C2 check-ins identified in the IDS logs. This chronological mapping allowed for the reconstruction of the full attack lifecycle: from the
initial delivery vector to the establishment of botnet persistence.
